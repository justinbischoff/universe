# The Making of Earth: Event Timeline

This is the list of events planned for the interactive page. It tells the story of the universe from Earth's point of view: where its atoms came from, how the planet formed, how life got started and grew complex, and how Earth's story will end.

Human history is deliberately left out except for one brief "Today" stop.

Dates are approximate. Many are active research topics, and the notes column says where the estimates are uncertain or disputed.

## Eras

The timeline is grouped into seven eras. Each era has its own colour on the page.

## Events

### The early cosmos

**1. The Big Bang**
- **When:** 13.8 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Space, time, and energy begin in a hot, dense state and start expanding. Protons and neutrons exist within the first second.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The protons made here are the hydrogen in Earth's oceans.
- **Scene:** A point of light that expands and cools from white to orange.

**2. The first nuclei**
- **When:** 3 to 20 minutes after the Big Bang
- **What happened:** The universe works like a fusion reactor for a few minutes. It ends up as about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, plus a trace of lithium.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Nothing heavier exists yet, so no carbon, oxygen, silicon, or iron. A rocky planet is impossible at this point.
- **Elements:** H, He, Li
- **Scene:** Protons and neutrons fusing into helium, with a bar showing the mass mix.

**3. The first light (cosmic microwave background)**
- **When:** 380,000 years after the Big Bang
- **What happened:** Neutral atoms form and light travels freely for the first time. We still detect this light as the cosmic microwave background. The "Dark Ages", about 100 million years with no stars, follow.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Small density ripples in this light are the seeds that gravity later grows into galaxies, including the Milky Way.
- **Scene:** A uniform orange glow at about 3,000 K, then the familiar all-sky CMB map as we see it today at 2.7 K.

**4. The first stars (Population III)**
- **When:** About 100–200 million years after the Big Bang
- **What happened:** Very massive stars made only of hydrogen and helium form. They live a few million years and explode. Their ultraviolet light reionizes the gas between galaxies.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** These stars made the first carbon and oxygen. Every later generation of stars starts out a little richer in heavy elements.
- **Elements:** C, O
- **Scene:** Blue giant stars switching on in the dark, carving bubbles of ionized gas, some exploding.

### The galaxy

**5. The Milky Way takes shape**
- **When:** About 13 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Gas and small protogalaxies merge to build our galaxy. Some of its stars are about 12 billion years old or older, such as the halo star HD 140283.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The galaxy is where Earth's raw materials are made, mixed, and gathered over the next 8 billion years.
- **Scene:** Scattered clumps of stars falling together into a spiral.

**6. A galactic collision (Gaia–Enceladus)**
- **When:** About 10 billion years ago (estimates range from about 8 to 11)
- **What happened:** A dwarf galaxy hits the young Milky Way. The Gaia space telescope identified its scattered stars in 2018 from their shared orbits.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Mergers stirred up gas and triggered bursts of star formation, which sped up the production of heavy elements.
- **Scene:** A dwarf galaxy spiralling in and being stretched into a stream of stars.

### Star forges

**7. Generations of supernovae**
- **When:** Repeatedly, from about 12 billion years ago until the Sun forms
- **What happened:** Massive stars explode as core-collapse supernovae and spread oxygen, silicon, magnesium, and calcium. Exploding white dwarfs (Type Ia supernovae) supply most of the iron.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** By mass, Earth is about 32% iron, 30% oxygen, 15% silicon, and 14% magnesium. Nearly all of it was made in stars that died before the Sun was born.
- **Elements:** O, Mg, Si, S, Ca, Fe
- **Scene:** A blue supergiant collapsing, then a layered blast shell with labels for oxygen, silicon, and iron.

**8. Dying stars release carbon**
- **When:** Ongoing, billions of years before the Sun
- **What happened:** Sun-like stars swell into red giants (the asymptotic giant branch) and shed their outer layers as planetary nebulae. These carry fresh carbon and nitrogen, plus slowly built heavy elements such as barium and lead.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** A large share of the carbon in living things, and much of the nitrogen in the air, came from these quiet stellar deaths, not from explosions.
- **Elements:** C, N, Ba, Pb
- **Scene:** A glowing ring nebula around a white dwarf.

**9. Neutron stars collide (kilonova)**
- **When:** Before 4.6 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Merging neutron stars make heavy elements through rapid neutron capture (the r-process). The 2017 event GW170817 was seen in both gravitational waves and light.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Gold, platinum, and uranium come from events like this. One study (Bartos & Márka, 2019) suggests a single merger about 80 million years before the Sun formed, around 1,000 light-years away, supplied a noticeable share of the solar system's heaviest elements. The uranium also helps heat Earth's interior.
- **Elements:** I, Pt, Au, U
- **Scene:** Two neutron stars spiralling together with gravitational-wave ripples, a flash, then gold-coloured ejecta.

### The solar system

**10. A supernova next door**
- **When:** About 4.568 billion years ago
- **What happened:** The Sun's birth cloud sat in a busy star-forming region. Short-lived radioactive aluminium-26 and iron-60, preserved in meteorites, show that nearby massive stars seeded the cloud around the time it collapsed.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Heat from aluminium-26 melted and dried out the first small bodies. That may be part of why the inner rocky planets formed relatively dry.
- **Elements:** ²⁶Al, ⁶⁰Fe
- **Scene:** A shock wave sweeping through a dark dust cloud, carrying aluminium-26, and a core starting to collapse.
- **Note:** Whether a supernova triggered the collapse, or only enriched the cloud, is still debated. Massive-star winds are another candidate source for aluminium-26.

**11. The Sun ignites**
- **When:** 4,567.3 million years ago
- **What happened:** The cloud collapses into a spinning disk around the young Sun. The oldest solids in the solar system, calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs) in meteorites, condense here. Their age defines "time zero" for the solar system.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Outside the snow line, water freezes into ice. Inside it, only rock and metal condense. This sets the recipe for the rocky inner planets.
- **Scene:** A cloud flattening into a disk, the Sun brightening, and a marked snow line.

**12. Jupiter carves the disk**
- **When:** Within about 1 million years of the Sun forming
- **What happened:** Jupiter's core grows to about 20 Earth masses quickly (Kruijer et al., 2017). It opens a gap that splits the disk into two reservoirs, a divide still visible in meteorite isotopes.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Jupiter limited how much water-rich material reached the inner disk and may have kept Mars small. Earth was built mostly from the dry inner reservoir.
- **Scene:** A disk with a gap, Jupiter orbiting in it, and labels for the dry inner and icy outer regions.

### Young Earth

**13. Earth assembles**
- **When:** About 4.54 billion years ago, over tens of millions of years
- **What happened:** Collisions between planetary embryos build Earth. Heat from impacts and radioactive decay melts it, and iron sinks to form the core.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The molten iron core later powers the magnetic field that shields the atmosphere from the solar wind.
- **Scene:** A glowing, magma-covered planet with impact flashes.

**14. Theia and the Moon**
- **When:** About 4.51 billion years ago
- **What happened:** A Mars-sized body, Theia, hits the young Earth. Debris in orbit gathers into the Moon, which starts out perhaps 15 times closer than today. A day lasts only a few hours.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The Moon steadies Earth's axial tilt, which keeps the climate relatively stable over billions of years. Its tides are still slowing Earth's spin.
- **Scene:** Theia striking, a debris ring forming, and the Moon gathering out of it.

**15. The first oceans**
- **When:** About 4.4 billion years ago
- **What happened:** 4.4-billion-year-old zircon crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia carry chemical traces of liquid water. Large impacts continue.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Earth had liquid water almost from the start, even though the young Sun was about 30% dimmer than today (the "faint young Sun" problem).
- **Scene:** A dark, mostly ocean planet under an orange haze, with occasional impacts.

### Life

**16. The first cells**
- **When:** About 4.2 to 3.5 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Chemistry becomes biology, possibly at hydrothermal vents on the seafloor. A 2024 genetic study (Moody et al.) estimates that the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) lived about 4.2 billion years ago. Fossil microbial mats in the Pilbara region of Australia confirm life by 3.48 billion years ago.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** LUCA already had DNA, ribosomes, and a membrane. Every later organism, including bacteria and trees, descends from it.
- **Scene:** Cells with membranes dividing near a hydrothermal vent chimney.
- **Note:** Earlier claimed evidence, such as 3.7-billion-year-old Greenland stromatolites, is disputed.

**17. Life learns to use sunlight**
- **When:** About 3.5 to 2.7 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Microbial mats build layered mounds called stromatolites in shallow seas. Cyanobacteria develop oxygen-producing photosynthesis.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** For hundreds of millions of years, dissolved iron and volcanic gases absorb the oxygen, so the oceans rust before oxygen builds up in the air.
- **Scene:** Stromatolites growing layer by layer in a sunlit shallow sea, releasing oxygen bubbles.
- **Note:** When oxygenic photosynthesis first appeared is debated. Estimates range from about 3.0 to 2.7 billion years ago or earlier.

**18. The Great Oxidation Event**
- **When:** About 2.4 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Oxygen finally accumulates in the atmosphere. Methane is destroyed, and Earth enters its first global glaciations (the Huronian). Banded iron formations settle out of seawater.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Oxygen makes an ozone layer possible and later fuels large, active life. Most iron ore mined today comes from these layers.
- **Scene:** A planet with rust-coloured land and ice spreading from the poles.

**19. Complex cells (eukaryotes)**
- **When:** About 2 to 1.6 billion years ago
- **What happened:** A microbe engulfs a bacterium that survives inside it and becomes the mitochondrion. The result is the eukaryotic cell, with a nucleus and internal compartments. Clear eukaryote fossils appear by about 1.65 billion years ago.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Every animal, plant, and fungus is built from eukaryotic cells. Mitochondria supply the extra energy that larger bodies need.
- **Scene:** A large cell with a nucleus, taking in a bacterium that becomes a mitochondrion.

**20. Cells team up (multicellularity)**
- **When:** About 1 billion years ago
- **What happened:** Multicellular life evolves several times. *Bangiomorpha*, a red alga about 1.05 billion years old, is the oldest known organism with distinct cell types and sexual reproduction.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Cells dividing up the work is the step that eventually leads to leaves, roots, and wood.
- **Scene:** Filaments of red algal cells swaying on a sunlit seafloor.

**21. Snowball Earth**
- **When:** About 720 to 635 million years ago (two major episodes)
- **What happened:** Ice reaches the tropics and may cover nearly the whole planet. Volcanic carbon dioxide builds up until the greenhouse effect breaks the ice.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Life survives in refuges. Soon after the thaw, oxygen rises again and large organisms appear.
- **Scene:** An almost fully frozen white planet.

**22. Animals arrive (Ediacaran and Cambrian)**
- **When:** About 575 to 520 million years ago. The Cambrian explosion starts about 539 million years ago.
- **What happened:** Soft-bodied Ediacaran organisms spread across the seafloor, followed by the Cambrian explosion, when most major animal groups appear with eyes, shells, and legs.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Burrowing animals mix the seafloor, and shells start locking carbon into limestone.
- **Scene:** Fern-like *Charnia* fronds and *Dickinsonia* give way to trilobites and *Anomalocaris*.

**23. Plants reach land**
- **When:** About 470 million years ago
- **What happened:** Fossil spores show small plants, related to liverworts, colonizing damp ground. The ozone layer now blocks most harmful UV on land.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Plants begin turning bare rock into soil, which eventually reshapes continents, rivers, and the atmosphere.
- **Scene:** A barren rocky shore where tiny green plants spread out from the water's edge.

**24. The first trees** *(highlight)*
- **When:** About 390 to 385 million years ago (Devonian)
- **What happened:** Plants evolve wood. *Wattieza* stood about 8 m tall with a palm-like crown. *Archaeopteris* had true wood, leafy branches, and deep roots. A fossil forest near Cairo, New York, about 386 million years old, is the oldest known forest (Stein et al., 2019).
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Deep roots break rock into soil and pull carbon dioxide from the air, which cools the planet and raises oxygen. Nutrient runoff may have contributed to Late Devonian ocean extinctions.
- **Scene:** A forest of *Wattieza* and *Archaeopteris* growing branch by branch at dusk.

**25. The coal forests (Carboniferous)**
- **When:** About 359 to 299 million years ago
- **What happened:** Swamp forests of giant clubmosses such as *Lepidodendron*, some about 30 m tall, cover the tropics. Oxygen reaches roughly 30%, enough for dragonflies like *Meganeura* with wingspans around 70 cm.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Huge amounts of wood were buried before it could decay and became coal. That buried carbon kept oxygen high and the climate cool.
- **Scene:** A misty swamp forest with *Lepidodendron* and *Calamites*, and a giant dragonfly.
- **Note:** The older idea that fungi could not yet break down wood is now disputed. Climate and tectonics probably explain most of the coal burial.

**26. The Great Dying (end-Permian extinction)**
- **When:** 252 million years ago
- **What happened:** The Siberian Traps eruptions release huge volumes of carbon dioxide. Oceans warm, lose oxygen, and acidify. Roughly 80 to 90% of marine species disappear.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Recovery takes millions of years. The survivors include the ancestors of dinosaurs and mammals.
- **Scene:** A reddish, hazy planet with glowing volcanic regions.

**27. The asteroid (Chicxulub)**
- **When:** 66 million years ago
- **What happened:** An asteroid about 10 km wide strikes the Yucatán Peninsula. Soot and dust darken the sky, and about three quarters of species die out, including all non-bird dinosaurs.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Mammals and flowering plants spread into the emptied world.
- **Scene:** An incoming asteroid, an impact flash, and a darkening planet.

**28. Today**
- **When:** Now
- **What happened:** Modern humans have existed for about 300,000 years, under 0.01% of Earth's history. If Earth's 4.54 billion years were squeezed into one day, humans would show up less than 6 seconds before midnight.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Nearly everything that made Earth habitable happened long before anyone was around. Microbes ran the planet for most of its history.
- **Scene:** Present-day Earth with clouds, ice caps, and city lights on the night side.

### The future

**29. Too bright for oceans**
- **When:** About 1 billion years from now
- **What happened:** The Sun brightens by about 10% per billion years. Faster weathering pulls carbon dioxide out of the air until most plants can't photosynthesize. Water vapour rises high into the atmosphere and is lost to space.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Complex life likely ends. Microbes may survive longer underground.
- **Scene:** A dry, salt-flat planet under a much brighter Sun.

**30. Andromeda draws near**
- **When:** Billions of years from now
- **What happened:** The Andromeda galaxy is approaching at about 110 km/s. A merger was long expected in about 4.5 billion years. A 2025 analysis of Hubble and Gaia data (Sawala et al.) puts the chance of a merger within 10 billion years at roughly 50%.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** Stars are so far apart that the Sun would almost certainly not collide with another star, but the night sky would change completely.
- **Scene:** Andromeda growing larger as it approaches, with the Sun's position marked in the Milky Way.

**31. The Sun becomes a red giant**
- **When:** About 5 to 7.6 billion years from now
- **What happened:** The Sun runs out of hydrogen in its core and swells to more than 200 times its current size, swallowing Mercury and Venus. Earth's orbit widens as the Sun loses mass, but tidal drag probably pulls Earth in anyway (Schröder & Smith, 2008).
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The planet that formed 4.5 billion years earlier most likely ends up vaporized in the Sun's outer layers.
- **Scene:** The Sun's red surface spreading across the sky and engulfing Venus, then Earth.

**32. Back to the stars**
- **When:** About 8 billion years from now
- **What happened:** The Sun throws off its outer layers as a planetary nebula and leaves behind an Earth-sized white dwarf.
- **Why it matters for Earth:** The cycle that built Earth starts over. Atoms that were once part of Earth's rocks, oceans, and trees may end up in new stars and planets.
- **Scene:** A planetary nebula carrying Earth's elements back into the galaxy. This mirrors event 8.

## Extra data shown on the page

Starting when the Sun forms, a small "vitals" panel shows rough conditions at each moment:

| Measure | Range shown | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Sun's brightness | About 71% of today at formation, about 10% brighter per billion years, roughly 2,700× at the red-giant peak | Standard solar evolution models (Gough, 1981 approximation) |
| Length of day | About 5 hours after the Moon formed, about 19 hours during the "boring billion", about 22 hours in the Devonian, 24 hours now | Tidal rhythmites, coral growth bands, and cyclostratigraphy (e.g., Meyers & Malinverno, 2018) |
| Moon distance | About 25,000 km at formation, rising to 384,400 km today | Tidal models and rhythmite records |
| Oxygen in air | Trace before 2.4 billion years ago, about 30% or more in the Carboniferous, 21% now | Geochemical proxy reconstructions |

All of these values are rough and are labelled as approximate on the page.

Besides the evenly spaced event track, the page has a thin bar drawn to scale, from the Big Bang to the Sun's death about 21.5 billion years after the Big Bang. It shows that:

- Earth exists only for the most recent third of cosmic history so far.
